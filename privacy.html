Bihana privacy policy

Bihana is a sunrise, sun and moon alarm clock for Android, made by Amrit Thapa. This page says what the app does with your information. Last changed: 21 October 2026.

In one sentence: your place is kept on your phone, for the sun and moon times; to show its name, the app sends it to OpenStreetMap, rounded to about a kilometre, and a city you type is sent there to find it; the app sends nothing else, and there is no account, no advertising and no tracking.

What the app keeps on your phone

  • Your alarms and their settings, including any sound file you chose for an alarm.
  • Your place, if you give the app one (by GPS or by typing a city), and the name of that place.
  • Your choices in Settings, and which tips you have closed.
  • A table of the coming months' sunrise, sunset and moon times for your place, so alarms can ring while the app is closed.
  • A log of when alarms were set and when they rang, so the app can tell you when an alarm last rang. It holds alarm times and the phone's state at the time (for example whether the screen was on), not your place.

Apart from the two place-name requests described below, the app sends none of this anywhere. The app has no server and no account. If you use Android's own backup to your Google account, or move to a new phone with Android's transfer, Android may copy the app's data as it does for most apps; that copy is yours, under your Google account, and the developer cannot see it. Uninstalling the app, or clearing its storage in Android's settings, deletes the copy on the phone.

What the app sends

Only two things, both to OpenStreetMap's place-name service (Nominatim, run by the OpenStreetMap Foundation, a non-profit registered in the United Kingdom), and only so the app can show the name of a place:

  1. Your place, to look up its name. When the app finds your place by GPS, it sends that place, rounded to two decimal places (about one kilometre), and gets back the name of the town or city. It asks again only when you have moved about 5 km or more, or when an earlier lookup got no answer; otherwise it uses the name it already has.
  2. A city you type, to find it. When you type a city, the text you typed is sent to find where that city is.

The app names itself in these requests ("Bihana", with the address of the app's web site and the app's email address), as OpenStreetMap asks of every app. Like every web request from a phone, they also carry Android's standard browser description, which names the phone model and the Android version, the phone's language, the app's own address inside the phone (https://localhost), and your internet address, which any website you visit sees. The app adds nothing else: no account, no advertising identifier, none of your alarms or contacts.

These requests are the only way the app can show where you are by name. To avoid them, give the app no place: clock alarms work without one, but sun and moon alarms need a place to be worked out.

OpenStreetMap's own handling of the requests it receives is described in the OpenStreetMap Foundation's privacy policy: https://osmfoundation.org/wiki/Privacy_Policy

Alarms, sun and moon times and the sky's colours are all worked out on the phone. None of them needs the internet.

What the app does not do

  • No account, sign-in or profile.
  • No advertising and no advertising identifiers.
  • No analytics, crash reporting or tracking of any kind.
  • No sale of your information, and no sharing beyond the two OpenStreetMap requests above.
  • No location history: the app keeps your current place and the place it last found a name for, not a record of where you have been.

Permissions, and why each is asked

  • Location (precise and approximate): to work out sunrise, sunset and moon times for where you are. Android asks for it when you tap "Use my location", or "Set location" or "Update" in Alarm health; you can type a city instead and never grant it. Once you have used your location, the app checks it again each time you open it or bring it back on screen (never in the background), so sun times follow you when you travel (if you allowed location only once, Android may ask again then); a typed city is never checked again.
  • Notifications and full-screen alarms: to show the ringing alarm over the lock screen, and the daily sky notices you switch on.
  • Exact alarms and starting after a restart: so alarms ring at their minute, also after the phone restarts.
  • Keeping the phone awake, vibration and a foreground service for sound: so the alarm can sound and vibrate until you stop it.
  • Internet: only for the place-name requests above.

Children

The app is a general alarm clock. It collects nothing from anyone, child or adult, beyond what is described above.

Changes

If the app ever starts sending anything else, this page will say so before that version is released, with the date at the top changed.
